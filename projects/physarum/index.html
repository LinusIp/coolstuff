how it works

A brainless blob that builds road networks

Physarum polycephalum is a single-celled slime mould with no nervous system, yet it finds short paths through mazes and, given oat flakes placed like the cities around Tokyo, grows a network strikingly close to the real rail map. Everything you see here comes from the same trick: lots of dumb parts, one shared chemical memory.

One agent, four rules

  1. Sense. Sample the trail map at distance d, straight ahead and at ±θ: F, FL, FR.
  2. Rotate. If F is strongest, keep going. If FL or FR wins, turn that way by the turn speed. If both sides beat the centre, pick one at random.
  3. Move. Step forward. The world wraps around like a torus.
  4. Deposit. Drop a little pheromone where you land.

The map remembers, then forgets

After every step the whole trail map is blurred a little (diffusion) and multiplied by a number just below one (decay):

T ← λ · ( (1 − k)·T + k·blur₃ₓ₃(T) ) + deposits

That is the entire model. Busy trails attract more agents, which makes them busier; quiet trails evaporate. Positive feedback plus forgetting prunes the chaos into a transport network: thick highways, thin capillaries, loops that keep everything connected. Nobody plans it.

Rival species

Each colour is a species that follows its own pheromone and treats the others' as a repellent (weight −repulsion). They carve out territories and braid around each other instead of merging.

Why it runs at a million agents

Every agent is one pixel of a 1024-wide float texture (x, y, heading, species). A fragment shader updates all of them in parallel; a second pass draws each agent as a single additive point into the trail map; a third blurs and decays it. The glow is the trail map's own mip-map pyramid. Nothing is ever read back to the CPU, and the population shrinks automatically if your GPU can't hold 55 fps.
