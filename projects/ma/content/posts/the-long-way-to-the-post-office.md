There are two roads from my workshop to the post office. The short one follows the river embankment, past the petrol station and the shuttered rice shop, and takes six minutes if the crossing is green. The long one climbs behind the shrine, cuts through the persimmon orchards on the east slope, and comes down past the school. It takes forty minutes.

For three years I have taken the long one every Thursday, carrying whatever I have bound that week, and I have slowly come to think of it as part of the work rather than a delay to it.

## The parcel is not the point

I repair books for a living: family registers, poetry anthologies that someone's grandmother annotated in pencil, cookbooks held together by rice starch and hope. When a book is finished I wrap it in plain paper, tie it with cotton tape and walk it down the hill. The customer never sees the walk. They see a parcel arrive on a Friday, open it, and find their book whole again.

But the walk is where I find out whether the work was good. Somewhere around the shrine steps, with the package under my arm, I remember the joint I hurried, the endpaper that did not quite sit flat. I have turned back twice. Both times I was right to.

A short road gets you there. A long road gives you time to find out whether you should have gone.

## What the slope keeps

The east slope changes on a schedule that is exact and entirely unannounced. In April the orchard floor goes white with blossom from the cherries the farmers planted as windbreaks. In June the path is closed for a fortnight by a mudslide that happens every June and surprises everyone. In late summer there is a week when the cicadas stop all at once, as if someone had lifted the needle from a record, and for a day or two the quiet is so complete you can hear the river from the top of the hill.

The school at the bottom of the slope keeps its own calendar. In spring, the first-years walk to class in a careful line with yellow hats. By midsummer the line has dissolved into knots of three and four. By autumn they run. Every Thursday I pass at the same moment the morning bell goes, and I have watched a whole cohort learn, in the space of one year, that they are allowed to be late.

In October the persimmons turn. They go from green to a yellow that looks unripe, and then, over about ten days, to the deep lacquered orange that means the farmers will start picking. I know this because I see it, every Thursday, a little further along. If I took the river road I would know it the way most of us know things now: as a fact, from somewhere, slightly too late.

## Four things the long way taught me

- **Distance is a filter.** Anything I still want to send after forty minutes of walking, I actually wanted to send.
- **Weather is information.** Wet ground on the slope means paste in the workshop will dry slowly tomorrow. I plan the week around the walk, not the other way round.
- **People talk when you are not hurrying.** Mr. Hayami, who owns the upper orchard, has told me more about grafting than any book. He would not have said a word to someone striding past.
- **Arriving tired is honest.** I reach the counter a little out of breath, the clerk can see I have come a long way, and we are both kinder for it.

## Against efficiency, a little

I do not want to make this into a philosophy. The short road is a perfectly good road. On wet weeks, or when a customer is waiting on a book for a funeral, I take it and feel no guilt at all. The point is not that slowness is a virtue. The point is that the time I save on the short road has to go somewhere, and when I look honestly at where it goes, the answer is usually: nowhere better.

The bookbinder who trained me used the word *ma* about the gutter of a book, the margin at the spine that nobody looks at but which decides whether a page will open flat. Too little and the text disappears into the fold. Too much and the book feels hollow. The walk is the gutter of my week. Nobody sees it. Everything opens properly because it is there.

## Thursday

Last week the clerk, who is young and moved here from the city in spring, asked why I don't use the new collection service. They come to your door, she said. You wouldn't have to carry anything.

I said I would think about it, which was true. I thought about it all the way home, the long way, past the orchard where Mr. Hayami's ladders were already leaning into the first trees. By the shrine I had decided. I will keep carrying things. It is one of the few parts of my week that cannot be made faster without being made into something else.[^1]

[^1]: To be fair to the collection service: it is excellent, and my neighbour, who is eighty-three and posts pickles to six grandchildren, considers it the finest civic achievement of the decade.
