The temple up the road is not famous. It has no gift shop and no entry fee, and the gravel garden behind the main hall is about the size of two parking spaces: pale granite chips, two stones, a strip of moss along the wall. The caretaker, Mrs. Ono, has raked it every morning for thirty-one years. This spring her knee stopped agreeing to it, and somehow I volunteered.

## The rake is heavier than it looks

It is a wooden rake with broad square teeth, the handle polished by three decades of the same palms. The idea is simple. Straight parallel lines across the open ground, like a still sea. Concentric rings around each stone, like ripples where something has fallen in. Where the rings meet the straight lines, a clean seam.

On my first morning I produced something that looked like a dog had run through it. The lines wandered. The rings were eggs. Where I had stepped backwards to admire my work, there were footprints.

Mrs. Ono watched from the veranda with her tea, and said nothing, and the next morning showed me how to walk backwards *without* looking at what I had done.

::: pull
You can't rake a straight line by looking at it. You have to look where you are going and trust your hands with where you have been.
:::

## What I have learned so far

- **The line is in your hips, not your arms.** You walk the line. The rake just follows.
- **Rings are easier than straights.** A circle forgives you; your error spreads out around it. A straight line shows every hesitation.
- **The seams are the hardest part.** Where two patterns meet, both have to give a little. Neither wins.
- **Early is better.** Before the sun is high the gravel is cool and slightly damp and holds an edge. By ten it is dry and the lines slump.

## The sound of it

Nobody warned me that raking would be loud. Granite chips against wooden teeth make a dry, continuous hush, something like surf heard through a wall, and on still mornings it fills the whole temple yard. Mrs. Ono says she can tell from the veranda whether I am concentrating. When my mind drifts, the rhythm breaks: a pause, a scrape, a hurried catch-up. When it is going well, the sound is so even that I stop hearing it.

I have started to think of the sound as the real measure of the work. A crooked line I can fix. A broken rhythm means I was somewhere else, and the line was only a symptom.

## Raking for no one

Hardly anyone sees the garden. A few neighbours walk their dogs through the temple grounds; the occasional cyclist stops to refill a bottle at the tap. By afternoon the wind and the birds and the odd fallen leaf have softened the lines, and the next morning I rake them again.

I asked Mrs. Ono, once, who the garden was for. She thought about it for long enough that I worried I'd been rude. Then she said that the stones were for looking at, and the gravel was for raking, and the raking was for the person with the rake. Then she went back to her tea.

I don't think she meant it as a lesson. She is not a teacher and dislikes being treated as one. But I have turned it over many times since. The gravel is not a picture of the sea. It is a record of a morning's attention, erased daily, which is why it never becomes precious, which is why you can do it badly without shame.

## Badly, and then slightly less badly

It is late July now. My lines are straighter. My rings are almost circles. Last week the seam on the east side came out clean for the first time, and I stood there for a ridiculous length of time looking at it, a forty-centimetre join between two patterns in a garden nobody visits.

By the next morning the wind had taken it. I raked it again. It wasn't as good. That was fine. Tomorrow is another go.
