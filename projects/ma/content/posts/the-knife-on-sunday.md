Every Sunday evening, after the dishes, I sharpen one knife. There are four in the kitchen and three in the workshop, so each blade comes round about once every seven weeks. None of them is ever dull. That is the point.

## Maintenance is the opposite of rescue

I learned this the expensive way. The first good knife I owned was a vegetable knife with a square tip, a gift on finishing my apprenticeship. I used it for two years without sharpening it properly, then took it to a stone in a panic when it began to crush tomatoes. Bringing back an edge that far gone took weeks of evenings. I scratched the face, rounded the bevel, and never quite got its first edge back.

A blade that is nearly sharp needs almost nothing. A few strokes on a fine stone, a few on the finishing stone, and it is new. A blade that has been allowed to fail needs to be partly rebuilt. The cost of neglect is not linear. It compounds.

## Ten minutes, roughly

My routine has settled into something I could do half asleep, which is close to how I want it.

1. **Soak the stone** in a basin until it stops releasing bubbles. This takes about as long as making tea, so I make tea.
2. **Find the angle.** Lay the bevel flat on the stone and feel for the moment it sits. Not a number: a sensation, like a key settling into a lock.
3. **Raise a burr.** Long, even strokes on the medium stone until a fine wire of metal can be felt, with a fingertip dragged *off* the edge, along the full length of the other side.
4. **Refine.** The same on the fine stone, lighter, fewer strokes, until the burr is barely there.
5. **Strop it away** on a strip of old newspaper laid over the bench, a handful of passes each side.
6. **Test** on the corner of a sheet of paper. A sharp edge slices without sound.

## Listening to the stone

There is a sound a blade makes when the angle is right: a steady, even hiss, like rain on a tin roof a long way off. When the angle wobbles the sound changes, scratchier, uneven, and you can correct it before you can see anything wrong. After a few years I realised I was sharpening more with my ears than my eyes.

The stone itself wears too. It slowly dishes in the middle, and every few months I flatten it against a coarse plate on the concrete step outside. Even the tool for caring for the tool needs care. This used to strike me as absurd. Now it seems simply true of everything that works.

## Where the stone came from

My first proper whetstone came from a woman who runs a hardware stall at the Saturday market in the next town. I had gone in for a stone and come out, forty minutes later, with a stone, a soaking basin, a lecture and a promise. The promise was that I would bring the vegetable knife back in a month so she could see what I had done to it.

When I did, she ran a thumbnail along the edge, looked at the bevel against the light, and said it was not good, but it was even, and even was the hard part. Good would come. I have taken her every new knife since. She has never once said one was good. Last spring she said *better*, and I went home and told nobody, because it would have sounded ridiculous, and was the best thing anyone said to me all year.

## Tools that are looked after

I don't think there is anything mystical in this. A sharp knife is safer, because it goes where you send it instead of skidding. It is faster, because you aren't fighting it. And it is kinder to food: a tomato cut cleanly keeps its juice; a crushed one bleeds it onto the board.

But I will admit to something beyond the practical. The Sunday knife has become a small border between weeks, a way of closing one and preparing for the next. The kitchen is quiet. The stone is wet. For ten minutes I attend to one object completely, and then I put it back where it lives, ready.

When I hand the nakiri to a guest now and they say it is the sharpest knife they have used, I want to tell them it isn't. It is just a knife that has never been allowed to become blunt. That is a much easier thing to make.
