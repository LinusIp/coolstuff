There is a kind of persimmon you cannot eat. Mr. Hayami grows two rows of them on the top terrace of his orchard, tall old trees with fruit shaped like acorns, and every October he gives me a crate because he knows I will do the one thing they are good for.

Picked from the tree, these astringent persimmons are so full of tannin that a single bite dries your mouth like a spoonful of chalk. Your tongue goes furry. Your cheeks pull in. Children who have been dared to try one remember it for years. And yet, given time, the same fruit becomes *hoshigaki*: dense, dark, chewy, sweet as dates, dusted with a white bloom of its own sugar. Nothing is added. The bitterness does not go anywhere. It simply changes its mind.

## The method, such as it is

The process is old and almost embarrassingly simple, which is why it is easy to get wrong.

1. **Peel them while firm.** Use a small knife and take the skin off in one thin spiral, leaving the calyx and a short T of stem to tie the string to.
2. **Dip them, briefly.** Some people pass each fruit through boiling water for a few seconds, or wipe it with a cloth dampened in strong spirit, to discourage mould. I do the first.
3. **Hang them in moving air.** Under the eaves, out of rain, where the wind from the valley can reach them. Space them so no two fruits touch.
4. **Wait a week. Then massage.** Every few days, roll each fruit gently between your fingers, softening the flesh inside without breaking the skin.
5. **Wait some more.** Four to six weeks, depending on the weather and on you.

## Nothing is wasted

The peels do not go in the bin. I spread them on a bamboo tray in the sun for a few days until they are leathery and dark, then give them to my neighbour, who buries them in the bran bed where she pickles her winter radishes. The dried skins carry a faint sweetness into the pickles and a colour like old gold. Her radishes are, by general agreement, the best on the street. She credits the persimmons. I credit her. Mr. Hayami, who grew the persimmons, credits nobody, and eats the radishes.

## The massage is the whole thing

Everything interesting happens in step four. You are not decorating the fruit. You are breaking down the firm interior so that it dries evenly into a soft, uniform paste rather than a hard shell around a wet heart. Too rough and you split the skin. Too timid and you get something like a sweet pebble.

There is no measurement for this. The only instrument is the thumb. In the first week the fruit is firm and cool and gives back nothing. By the third week it is leathery outside and yields like a ripe plum within. By the fifth it feels like a small, warm animal. I cannot describe the right pressure to anyone, but I have never once got it wrong after the first year, which tells me it is real knowledge, just not the kind that goes into sentences.

## What the eaves know

People ask whether the drying could be sped up. It could. There are machines. But a persimmon dried in a cabinet at a constant temperature tastes like candy: correct and uninteresting. The ones under my eaves dry in the long cold nights and the brief warm afternoons, and they take on something from both. They go slightly smoky from the neighbour's leaf fires. They taste, I swear, of the particular autumn they hung through.

I find this almost unbearably moving, which may say more about me than about fruit.

## The white bloom

Around the fifth week a fine white powder appears on the surface, as if the fruit had been out in a frost. Visitors sometimes think it has gone mouldy and look at me with concern.[^1] It is sugar, drawn out from inside and crystallised on the skin. It means you are nearly done. When I see it I stop massaging and leave them alone for the last few days, the way you stop stirring a sauce that has come together.

## Giving them away

I keep about a third. The rest go out in December, wrapped in paper, to the people who have helped me through the year: the clerk at the post office, the woman at the market who sold me my first whetstone, Mr. Hayami himself, who receives his own persimmons back transformed and pretends every year to be surprised.

We eat them slowly, in thin slices, with a cup of green tea. It is the least efficient sweet I know. Six weeks of hanging, dozens of small attentions, all to arrive at something you could, in principle, buy. But you couldn't, not really. You could buy a dried persimmon. You couldn't buy this one, which hung in this wind, which I touched on these mornings, which was bitter and then wasn't.

[^1]: Real mould is fuzzy, grey-green and smells of cellars. The sugar bloom is powdery, white, and smells of nothing but persimmon. If in doubt, rub a little between your fingers: sugar dissolves, mould smears.
