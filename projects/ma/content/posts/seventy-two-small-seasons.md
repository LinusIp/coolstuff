Four seasons is a blunt instrument. Anyone who has lived through a real year knows that "autumn" covers at least three different moods, and that the week the rice is cut has almost nothing in common with the week the first frost silvers the fields.

The old calendar knew this. Beneath the familiar four, it divides the year into twenty-four solar terms, and each of those into three smaller seasons called *kō*, of roughly five days each. Seventy-two seasons in all, each named for something you could see, hear or feel if you stepped outside at the right moment.

## A calendar you have to go outside to read

The names are not abstractions. They are observations, written down so long ago that some no longer quite match the weather, and so plain they read like notes left on a kitchen table:

- Dew glistens white on the grass.
- The wagtails sing.
- The swallows leave.
- Thunder lowers its voice.
- Insects close up their burrows.

That middle one, *the swallows leave*, is this week. So this morning, before opening the workshop, I walked down to the bridge where a colony has nested under the concrete for as long as I have lived here, and I stood there with a flask of tea for twenty minutes, waiting.

::: pull
Five days is short enough to notice and long enough to miss.
:::

## What I found

The nests were empty. A few stray feathers clung to the mud cups, and the river had that particular early-autumn clarity, as if the water had been rinsed. No swallows. They had gone, sometime in the past week, without telling me. The calendar was right, and I was a few days late.

I was surprised how much this pleased me. A forecast tells you what will happen; this is something different, an appointment. It says: around now, this will be true, come and see. Missing it doesn't break anything. You simply learn the size of your inattention, measured in days.

## Why five days

I have come to think the length is the clever part. A month is too long to feel; it blurs into "lately". A single day is too precise; it turns noticing into a chore. Five days is the span of a short trip, or of a cold coming and going. It is short enough that if you are not paying attention you will miss a season entirely, and long enough that if you are, you can catch it with ordinary effort.

Since spring I have kept a small notebook: one line per *kō*, written on the day it begins, saying what I actually saw. Some lines agree with the old names. Many don't. The valley has its own timing, and the climate has shifted since those names were written. The notebook is becoming a calendar of this place in particular, which is, I think, the only kind worth keeping.

## From the notebook

A few lines from this summer, copied out as written, to show how plain it is:

> *Early July.* Frogs in the flooded field below the school, so loud at dusk I closed the window to hear the radio.
>
> *Late July.* First cicada of the year at 5:40 in the morning. By noon, hundreds.
>
> *Mid August.* Red dragonflies over the rice, low, all facing the same way into the wind.
>
> *Early September.* Dew heavy enough on the orchard grass to soak my shoes through. The old name for this week is about dew. For once we agree.

None of this is remarkable. That is the point. The calendar does not ask for remarkable. It asks you to look, every five days or so, and write down what is there.

The surprise has been how much the notebook changes the looking. Knowing I will have to write a line on Thursday, I notice things on Tuesday. The first cold morning, the day the persimmon leaves start to redden at the edges, the evening the frogs go quiet. Without the appointment, they would happen anyway, and I would simply be indoors.

Next week, the thunder lowers its voice. I will be listening.
