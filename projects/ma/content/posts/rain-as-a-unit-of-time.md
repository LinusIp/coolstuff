The rainy season arrived on the ninth this year, almost to the day the radio said it would, and it has not paused since. Not a storm, nothing dramatic, just a soft grey continuousness, like a sound you stop hearing until it stops.

For a bookbinder this is the slow season. Paste won't set. Paper swells and refuses to lie flat. Boards warp overnight if you leave them unweighted. There is no point fighting it, so I stop trying to finish things and start doing the work that does not need to dry: sorting, mending small tears, cutting tape, reading.

## Measuring by water

Somewhere in the second week I noticed I had stopped thinking in hours. Instead I was thinking in rains.

There is the rain you can hear on the roof but not see from the window. There is the rain that blurs the far ridge but leaves the near orchard sharp. There is the rain that fills the stone basin by the door in an afternoon, and the heavier rain that fills it before lunch. "I'll go out when this one eases" has become a real unit of time in my house, more reliable than the clock, because it is what actually decides when I go out.

::: pull
A clock tells you when. Rain tells you whether.
:::

## A short vocabulary of rain

The language I grew up with has more words for rain than I ever needed in the city. Out here I use most of them in a week. A few I have come to rely on:

- ***Kirisame***, mist-rain. So fine it doesn't fall so much as hang. You notice it only when your sleeve is wet.
- ***Kosame***, light rain. An umbrella is optional and nobody bothers.
- ***Niwaka-ame***, a sudden shower. It arrives from a clear sky and leaves before you've found shelter, mostly to make a point.
- ***Samidare***, the long rain of early summer. The old poets used it for any steady grey weather that would not end, and for moods of the same kind.
- ***Yūdachi***, the evening downpour of high summer. Loud, warm, brief. Afterwards the road steams.

The rainy season itself is *tsuyu*, written with the characters for plum and rain, 梅雨, because it comes as the plums ripen.[^1] I like that the name points at the fruit rather than the weather. It says: something is being made in all this wet.

## Listening as work

I keep the workshop window open a hand's width, even when it means drying the sill twice a day. The sound of the rain changes with the weight of the drops and with what they land on: hiss on the gravel, patter on the persimmon leaves, a low tock on the overturned bucket by the wall. After a few weeks I can tell from my bench, without looking up, whether the afternoon will clear.

This sounds like a small talent, and it is. But it is one I would never have developed if I had shut the window and switched on a light and pretended it was any other month.

## What the season is for

I used to resent June. Six weeks of damp, the futons never quite dry, mould blooming on the back of the bathroom door. I still don't love it. But I have come to see it as the year's long exhale: the time when the valley closes in on itself and the urgent things wait.

The rain will stop in mid-July, abruptly, the way it always does, and the cicadas will start the same afternoon as if someone had flipped a switch. The paste will set again. I will have more orders than I can bind. And for a while I will miss this, the grey, the quiet, the permission not to finish anything at all.

[^1]: There is a second reading of the same characters, *baiu*, used mostly by weather forecasters. Nobody in the valley says it out loud.
