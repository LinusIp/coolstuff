The woman who taught me bookbinding had a habit that drove me quietly mad for the first year of my apprenticeship. Before trimming any page, she would lay her thumb along the inner margin, beside the spine, and hold it there for a moment. Then she would either nod and cut, or shake her head and move the guide a hair's breadth outward.

She never used a ruler for this. When I asked how wide the margin should be, she said: *as wide as it needs to be for the book to breathe*. I wrote this down in my notebook as if it were an answer.

## Ma is not empty

The word she used was *ma*. It is usually translated as "space", "gap", or "pause", and each of those is correct and each misses something. A gap is an absence. *Ma* is closer to an interval with a shape: the silence between two notes that makes them a phrase rather than two sounds, the step back from a painting that lets you see it, the beat in a conversation where the other person decides whether to tell you the true thing.

In a book, the most important *ma* is the gutter, the margin at the spine. Nobody looks at it. Everyone feels it. Too narrow and the text vanishes into the fold, and the reader has to wrestle the book open. Too wide and the pages feel thin, as if the content had been stretched to fill a format. When it is right, a book lies open in your hands without being held, and you forget that it is an object at all.

## Pauses that are part of the sentence

It took me years to notice that my teacher spoke the way she trimmed. She left long silences in conversation, longer than was comfortable, and in those silences people said things. Customers who had come in to drop off a broken album would stand at the counter and, into her quiet, explain that it had been their father's, that he had died in the winter, that they had not opened it since. She did not prompt them. She simply did not fill the space.

I tried to copy this and failed for a long time. My pauses were either too short, as if I were waiting for my turn, or too long, as if I had forgotten the question. The difference, I eventually understood, was that she was not *waiting* in her pauses. She was listening. A pause that is merely empty feels like a hole. A pause that is full of attention feels like room.

## The last lesson

On my final day as her apprentice, she gave me no exercise. She sat me down at the long bench with a finished book, one of hers, and asked me to tell her what was wrong with it.

I turned it over for nearly an hour. The sewing was perfect. The boards were square. The paper had been chosen for the weight of the ink. I could find nothing, and said so, expecting to be told I had missed something obvious.

Instead she nodded and said that was the right answer, but for the wrong reason. There was nothing wrong with it, she said, because there was nothing in it that asked to be noticed. A good book should disappear into the reading. The margins, the gutter, the space above the running head: they were all there to be unseen. If I ever made a book where someone admired the margins, I would have made them too clever.

I still think about that. Most of what she taught me was how to make room, and then how to get out of the way of it.

## Three places I look for it now

1. **On the page.** Before I commit to a margin I close my eyes and hold the book open with one hand. If my thumb hides any text, the gutter is too narrow.
2. **In a day.** I keep the half hour after lunch empty, no work and no reading. It is astonishing how much of the afternoon's work gets solved in it.
3. **In a reply.** When someone tells me something difficult, I count one slow breath before answering. It is almost never too long.

## The circle

On the first morning of this journal I painted the circle you see at the top of this page. The tradition is to do it in a single breath, in a single stroke, without correction. Mine is not a good one. The brush lifted too early and the end is ragged.

But it has the thing my teacher would have looked for. The gap where the stroke does not quite close is not a mistake. It is where the circle breathes. I have kept it above my desk all spring, a reminder that the space left open is not the part you failed to fill. It is the part that lets everything else be seen.
